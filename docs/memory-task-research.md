# What is worth turning into a memory?

Research reviewed on 2026-11-05. The strongest reason to save a moment is often a detail that would otherwise disappear. A chipped mug can recall a morning ritual. A group's unusual directions to a familiar place can recall who first gave them. Each starter task pairs something concrete to photograph with a question about what it means.

## What people value

The findings below are paraphrases from primary research. The task examples and assignment rules are Memorygram design decisions. The evidence covers reported sources of meaning, past memories, household keepsakes and experimental measures of enjoyment. A Memorygram-specific ranking of task preferences still requires research with its users.

| Evidence | What it suggests for tasks |
| --- | --- |
| In an open-ended survey of nearly 19,000 adults across 17 advanced economies, family was the most commonly mentioned source of meaning in most places surveyed. Other answers included work, friends and interests, with substantial variation between societies. [Pew Research Center, 2021](https://www.pewresearch.org/global/2021/11/18/what-makes-life-meaningful-views-from-17-advanced-economies/) | Invite people to choose whom they call their people. A meal learned from a relative, a chosen family's ritual and a solo hobby all deserve prompts. |
| In four studies of rediscovery, people underestimated how interested they would later be in records of everyday life. The gap was particularly apparent for ordinary experiences. [Zhang et al., 2014](https://www.hbs.edu/ris/Publication%20Files/Rediscovery_91a38887-12e7-4c5c-a60d-866c0e4a95c4.pdf) | Assign a task about the usual cup, sound or saying. Ask for the detail that future-you might forget. |
| Across eight studies, ordinary and unusual experiences both contributed to happiness, and their relative importance varied with age and perceived time ahead. [Bhattacharjee and Mogilner, 2014](https://doi.org/10.1086/674724) | Pair one familiar ritual with one small first or experiment. Everyone receives both kinds and chooses which to try. |
| A home field study found meaningful objects in everyday use. People kept things whose significance came from their personal associations. [Petrelli, Whittaker and Brockmeier, 2008](https://eprints.whiterose.ac.uk/id/eprint/3773/) | Let a utensil, trophy made from scraps or well-used object stand in for a person. The caption explains why that particular object matters. |
| Two experiments found that simultaneously experiencing the same taste amplified participants’ evaluations in both pleasant and unpleasant directions. The studies tested a narrow setting. [Boothby, Clark and Bargh, 2014](https://clarkrelationshiplab.yale.edu/sites/default/files/files/BoothbyClarkBargh%281%29.pdf) | Give the group a specific, low-pressure thing to notice at the same time. Ask each person what caught their attention. Chat also lets friends coordinate from different places. |
| In a study with 82 adults aged 60–86, music often brought to mind activities, specific people and places. The participants described the memories in their own words. [O’Shea et al., 2025 issue](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12377674/) | A solo prompt can start with a song and end with an object from the scene it evokes. Users can participate without uploading recordings. |
| Across experiments, a spatial recall exercise elicited more perceptual detail when people described personal events. This concerned the *way people recalled an event*. [Sheldon et al., 2019](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6716376/) | Ask for a reflection, a leaf shape or the landmark someone uses to give directions. Location permission helps choose a prompt. The memory comes from what the person notices. |
| In three field experiments and six lab experiments, photography could increase enjoyment when it helped people engage with a positive experience. Interference with the activity limited the benefit. Separate experiments found that taking a photo with sharing in mind could raise concerns about presentation and reduce enjoyment. [Diehl, Zauberman and Barasch, 2016](https://doi.org/10.1037/pspa0000055) · [Barasch, Zauberman and Diehl, 2018](https://doi.org/10.1093/jcr/ucx112) | Ask for one photo and a short caption after the activity. A sketch or object is enough. Save each result privately for its participants. |

## How the research changes an assignment

Each new space receives three different jobs. The 23-prompt starter catalog contains six solo prompts, six group prompts and eleven place prompts usable by either audience. Each task has a five- or ten-minute estimate and a free way to finish. A member can choose any open task. Saving its photo and caption completes it once for everyone.

| Assignment | Pocket Chapter example | With others example |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Keep a trace of ordinary life | **The usual, please.** Show the chipped edge of a favorite mug and say what you would miss about that ritual. | **Our unofficial tradition.** Ask how a recurring group habit began and preserve the competing origin stories. |
| Make or notice something new | **Keep the first draft.** Try something on scrap paper and save one thing the first attempt taught you. | **Same minute, two stories.** Compare the first thing two friends noticed, in person or on a call. |
| Anchor the memory to a place | **The unofficial postage stamp.** Design a stamp for the nearby town using a detail worth remembering. | **Postcard from right here.** Pick a detail a visitor would miss and address a line to a friend who would recognize it. |

The baseline place pool works at home, from a seat, or across a call. The location option changes only the third assignment. It first looks for a named landmark, then a nearby mapped town or city, then a local time-of-day cue. A place name is shown before creation so the user can decide whether to include it in the group's prompt. On native devices the system geocoder can supply a town when the offline map has no match.

## Geographic sources and limits

The app bundles names and rounded coordinates for 7,295 populated places drawn from Natural Earth 1:10m Populated Places Simple v5.1.2. The extract keeps latitude, longitude and name, rounds the public place centers to three decimal places and excludes historical places and stations. A center qualifies when its estimated distance plus the reported location uncertainty is within 8 km. The selection describes proximity to a mapped center. Municipal boundaries, routes, access conditions and opening hours require other data. [Natural Earth dataset](https://www.naturalearthdata.com/downloads/10m-cultural-vectors/10m-populated-places/) · [Public-domain terms](https://www.naturalearthdata.com/about/terms-of-use/)

Three site-specific prompts take priority within 2.5 km, including uncertainty. Their reference points come from published geographic records. Each task offers a way to participate from the user's current spot or from memory. [Crissy Field, US National Park Service](https://www.nps.gov/planyourvisit/event-details.htm?id=DACA47F3-D4CE-0BC9-C30A70A928969D34) · [Reflecting Pool area, US National Park Service](https://npgallery.nps.gov/AssetDetail/d29fbb68-fabb-4fcc-af49-a953e5fb20b7) · [Cubbon Park, Central Ground Water Board](https://www.cgwb.gov.in/cgwbpnm/public/uploads/documents/1687415901282344779file.pdf)

## What to learn from Memorygram users

A small diary study could test the core assumption directly. Have solo users and existing friend groups try an ordinary-life prompt and a discovery prompt, with the order varied. Ask which they wanted to do, which they actually did and which saved memory they chose to reopen a week later. Those are three different outcomes.

For location, ask participants whether naming their town made a prompt feel more personal, whether they would share that detail with this group, and what they did when the suggested place felt wrong. Compare responses from people who stayed home, met in person or participated remotely. Their own words and the memories they chose to revisit would guide the next catalog revision.

## Friendship quests by age

Research reviewed 2026-11-09. Findings below are paraphrases. The four age bands, the individual prompts and the median rule are editorial choices. Direct studies of Memorygram users would be needed to estimate how consistently friends in each range prefer these activities.

- In Pew’s open-ended survey across 17 advanced economies, friendship and community frequently ranked highly among 18–29-year-olds. Work was particularly prominent among 30–49-year-olds, and both family and occupation featured among 50–64-year-olds. The middle collections use exchanges of practical skills, reunions and shared projects that friends can enjoy across different household and career situations. Pew used these four adult age bands; the app’s youngest collection also accommodates younger friendship groups. [Pew Research Center, 2021](https://www.pewresearch.org/short-reads/2021/11/23/what-makes-life-meaningful-globally-answers-sometimes-vary-by-age/)
- Pew’s 2024 survey of US teens found that 63% said social platforms gave them room to show their creative side, while 31% felt pressure to post popular content. Under-30 prompts borrow the creative formats, with a private group as the audience. The survey covered ages 13–17, so extending the idea to people in their twenties is a design hypothesis. [Pew Research Center, 2025](https://www.pewresearch.org/wp-content/uploads/sites/20/2025/04/PI_2025.04.22_teens-social-media-mental-health_REPORT.pdf)
- Across eight studies, ordinary experiences became relatively more important as people aged. This informed prompts about recurring rituals, well-used tools and familiar streets. Novel challenges also appear throughout the collections. [Bhattacharjee and Mogilner, 2014](https://doi.org/10.1086/674724)
- Experiments on social partners found that changing perceived time horizons could shift preferences for familiar companions. Two friends comparing recollections of the same afternoon draws on the value of an established relationship. A range selector gives younger friends the same choice. [Fung, Carstensen and Lutz, 1999](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/10632147/)
- AARP’s survey of players aged 50+ identified differing motivations, including enjoyment, challenge and mastery. That evidence informed puzzle design, rule-making and teaching games in the older collections. The survey represents players, so its findings support variety rather than a population-wide prediction. [AARP Research, 2023](https://www.aarp.org/pri/topics/social-leisure/activities-interests/2023-gamers-50-plus/)
- The music-evoked memory and household keepsake studies above support concrete cues for conversation. The 30–49 and 50–64 collections also offer short skill exchanges and speculative design activities. Their precise cutoffs are editorial and can be overridden.

Each band has 25 distinct titles. The group range is computed from members’ current ages at creation. Missing birthdays select All ages. For a tight cluster the middle age (mean of the middle pair for even groups) supplies the range. A group spanning three or more bands, or whose middle pair falls in different bands, gets All ages. Birthdays are stored in owner-only profile records. The group calculation returns only a band. The selected range travels with group creation and, for nearby suggestions, with the model request.
